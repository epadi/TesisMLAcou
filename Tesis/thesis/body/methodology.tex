\section{Experimental measurement configuration}


The experimental container is a cubic box with a side length of 1 m and a volume close to $1\,\mathrm{m}^3$. The concept is shown in Figure \ref{mesurementbox}(a). Three of its six walls are made of porcelain tiles with a thickness of 1 cm, one wall and the floor are made of solid concrete, and the remaining wall used for observation is made of acrylic with a thickness of 0.5 cm. The clear acrylic sheet allows direct observation of the movement mechanism inside the box. Unlike the other walls, which are permanently bonded, this acrylic wall can be removed to provide access to the interior of the measurement box.

The movement mechanism inside the box consists of a robotic arm mounted at the center of the floor. The arm has four degrees of freedom (DOF) and is used to control the position and orientation of a cube with a side length of 20 cm attached to its end. The cube is made of plastic, with walls 2 mm thick. For the measurements, a mono speaker is placed at the center of one wall to generate the acoustic stimulus inside the system. On the opposite wall, four capacitive microphones are positioned 15 cm away from each corner to capture the acoustic response of the measurement box. The measurement box was constructed inside a storage room and is shown in Fig. \ref{mesurementbox}(b).

\section{The Audio-Location Dataset for Model Training and Testing}

The dataset was collected over twenty-six recording sessions. In each session, the robotic arm maintained a fixed base angle and positioned the cube at specific coordinates and orientations. The initial orientation of the cube was set such that its sides were parallel to the measurement box walls. The variables modified during each measurement were the cube’s location and orientation. For each location, nine different orientations were recorded. After completing these nine measurements, the robotic arm lowered the cube by decreasing its position along the Z axis, and the recording process was repeated for the new location. This procedure continued until the cube reached the bottom of the measurement box.

Audio from four channels was recorded. The acoustic signal was emitted four times, and the response was captured one microphone at a time. Each recording session consisted of 270 recordings, each containing four channels. A movement of 10 degrees in the base of the arm define the begining of a new recording session, with all the recording sessions producing a cylindrical envelope for the measurement area.

The coordinates of each measurement, obtained from the robotic arm, are shown in Fig. \ref{xyzlocations}. Each blue column in the three-dimensional plot corresponds to one recording session, and each point represents one of the nine orientations captured at that location. The total number of sessions was limited by the rotational constraint of the robotic arm’s base, which allowed only 270 degrees of movement.

The source audio signal was created by extracting an arbitrary 0.3-second segment from an acoustic melody. This type of audio was selected because of its suitability for acoustic environment recognition, as demonstrated by the melodies used in \cite{RIR05}. The signal's spectrum extends to 10 kHz, with higher-magnitude components concentrated at low frequencies. Before the source signal is played, the recording process begins by opening the capture window for one second. The signal is recorded four times, once for each microphone. After these recordings are complete, the cube is moved to the next orientation. This process is repeated until all nine orientations at that location have been recorded, after which the cube is moved to a new location.



\section{The Audio-Location Processing Pipeline}


Figure \ref{fig:method} shows the processing pipeline for the acoustic signals. The microphone array in the measurement box records four signals, which form the four audio channels used as input to the pipelines. In the first step, AudAlign, the signals are aligned to compensate for variation in their start times caused by the operating system's process scheduler. Alignment is performed by exploiting the equal distances between the source and each microphone; therefore, the magnitude of the direct sound can serve as a reference. During the same step, signal integrity is validated. Each recording is divided into a main segment and a tail (the reverberation segment). The signal power is calculated from the main segment, and the tail duration is measured. Recordings whose signal power and tail duration fall within predefined limits are considered valid and included in the dataset. The resulting dataset is used in four scenarios: two use CNN-based pipelines, and two use ESN-based pipelines. The CNN-based pipelines require the time-series data to be converted into image representations, whereas the ESN-based pipelines use a transformation from audio to spectral sequences. In addition, beamforming is used to address acoustic superposition by dividing the room into four regions and representing the reverberation originating from each region in a separate set of channels. These channels are also provided as input to the CNN and ESN estimators.

\section{Response Variables and evaluation metrics}

The networks in this research are configured as regression models, the outputs are the predicted coordinates (X, Y, Z) and orientation of the cube in the room. The response variables are the error between the predicted and actual values of these outputs, which are calculated as mean of the absolute difference between the predicted and actual values as shows in equation \ref{eq:mae}. The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is used as the performance metric for evaluating the models. The MAE is a widely used metric for regression tasks, as it provides a clear measure of the average magnitude of errors in predictions, without considering their direction. Lower MAE values indicate better model performance, as they reflect smaller discrepancies between predicted and actual values \cite{friedmanprocess}.


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mae}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
\end{equation}


The other metric used for evaluating a regression model  way to evaluate the model is to measure correlation between the real and the predicted values12. The
most well-known method for this is Pearson’s correlation coefficient, defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pearson}
r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} \in [-1, 1],
\end{equation}

This metric provides additional insight into the model's performance, complementing the MAE by indicating how well the predictions follow the trend of the actual values \cite{friedmanprocess}.


\section{Data Recording for Model Evaluation}

To evaluate the performance of the models, a five-fold cross-validation approach is used. In this approach, the dataset is divided into five subsets, and the model is trained and tested five times, each time using a different subset as the test set and the remaining subsets as the training set. This process allows for a more robust evaluation of the model's performance and helps to mitigate any potential bias that may arise from using a single train-test split. The total 7020 samples are divided into five folds, with each fold containing near to 1,404 samples (A 80:20 ratio). The performance of the model is evaluated considering the Mean Absolute Error (MAE). \section{Tool Used for Models Comparison}

In the literature, the use of parametric tests to compare machine learning models is often discouraged because the assumptions they require cannot always be taken for granted for learning algorithms \cite{demsar2006statistical}; nonparametric tests are therefore preferred. Two nonparametric approaches recommended for comparing regression models are the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the Friedman test. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test estimates whether the differences between two models are statistically significant with respect to a specific evaluation metric. However, it is not recommended for comparing several models because doing so increases the risk of Type I errors. The Friedman test is used to compare more than two models \cite{friedmanrecomendation}.

Because performance metrics are obtained through cross-validation, the observations are not independent: the training sets overlap across folds. This dependence complicates the comparison of multiple models using the Friedman test and other traditional statistical tests \cite{demsar2006statistical}.

On this problem, Bayesian approaches have been proposed as a more suitable alternative for comparing multiple models when the observations are not even independent, as they can properly account for the uncertainty and dependencies in the data \cite{benavoli2017time,  mollica2017bayesian, calvo2018bayesian}. These approaches provide a coherent probabilistic framework to model the rankings of the learing algorithms based on their performance metrics and make inferences about their relative performance.

In this work, we adopt the Bayesian framework described in \cite{calvo2018bayesian} to compare the four regression models base on the MAE as the mainperformance metrics obtained through cross-validation. This method allows us to properly account for the dependencies and uncertainties in the performance metrics obtained through five fold cross-validation. The procedure to compare multiple regression models using the Bayesian approach is implemented with the steps decribed in \cite{calvo2018bayesian} and detailed as follows:

1) Create a matrix of performance metrics obtained through cross-validation for each regression model, where each row corresponds to a fold and each column corresponds to a model and from this matrix a ranking matrix is derived, where each element represents the rank of a model in a specific fold based on its performance metric.

2) Use the Plackett-Luce (PL) model as framework to model the probability distribution of the machine learning model rankings:

Parameter Interpretation: The parameters are a weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_n)$ where $\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1$ and $w_i > 0$. Each weight $w_i$ directly represents the marginal probability that algorithm $i$ is ranked in first position (i.e., is the best algorithm).

Likelihood Formulation: Under the PL model, the probability assigned to a specific ranking $\sigma = (\sigma(1), \dots, \sigma(n))$ is calculated as: 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:PL}
P_{PL}(\sigma | \mathbf{w}) = \prod_{j=1}^n \frac{w_{\sigma(j)}}{\sum_{l=j}^n w_{\sigma(l)}}
\end{equation}


3) A prior distribution is specified for the weight vector $\mathbf{w}$. A common choice is the Dirichlet prior:

$$P(\mathbf{w}) = \text{Dir}(\mathbf{w}; \boldsymbol{\alpha})$$ where $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ are hyperparameters3.

where $\alpha_i > 0$ are the hyperparameters of the prior, and set to $\alpha_i = 1$ for a non-informative prior.

4) Compute the posterior distribution of the weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ given the observed rankings. Using Bayes' theorem, the posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior:

$$P(\mathbf{w} | R) \propto  P( R|\mathbf{w}) \cdot P(\mathbf{w})$$

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Bayes}
P(\mathbf{w} | R)  \propto \prod_{\sigma \in R} P_{PL}(\sigma | \mathbf{w}) \cdot P(\mathbf{w})
\end{equation}

Because this posterior distribution lacks a closed-form analytical expression, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling is employed to draw a representative set of weight vector samples from the posterior distribution $P(\mathbf{w} | R)$

5) Once the posterior samples of the weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ are obtained, counting the times the weight $w_i$ is the largest among all weights allows us to estimate the posterior probability that algorithm $i$ is the best.
