\section{CNN Estimator}

The related work presented in seccion 2.6.2 provide insights respect the potential use of neural network to estimate the location of an object using acoustic echoes, and considering the especific findings in \cite{echosense} is that a metric is extrapolated for the magnitude of estimation error. Due the fact that the dataset was new and unexplored, the model choose to establish a reference point was a CNN, this with the premise that a CNN have enogh representational capacity to learn from an especific acostic trasformation the function that maps the input features to espatial coordinates, providing valuable information if our original goals can be achieved with a more restricted network as is the case of an ESN. 

\subsection{Log-Mel spectrogram as images}

To train a CNN on the dataset, a Log-Mel transformation is applied to the audio signals to generate spectrograms that can be used as network input. Each audio signal is 35,000 samples long. A short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is computed using a 2,048-sample window and a hop size of 512 samples, yielding 68 frames, as described in Equation \ref{eq:fframes}. An additional Mel filter bank compresses the spectral data into 128 bands, making it easier for machine-learning models to process \cite{log_mel_convolutional}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fframes}
\text{Frequency frames} = \frac{signal\_length}{window\_step} 
\end{equation}

Finally to reduce feature variance in the mel bands a logaritmic trasformation is perform. The resulting Log-Mel matrix is transform to images of 570x370 pixels per location-orientation as the one shown in Figure \ref{spectogram4ch}.

\subsection{CNN Architecture}

The model architecture selected for the task of classifying and estimating the location–orientation of the cube inside the measure box was the ResNet‑18 network with a total of 11,689,512 trainable parameters, chosen for its robustness, simplicity, and suitability for inference in resource‑constrained environments \cite{Resnetwhy}. Two modifications were applied to the standard network structure. First, the input layer was extended to accept a stack of four RGB images, one corresponding to each audio channel. Second, for the regression task, the softmax output layer was replaced with a single output feature using a sigmoid activation function.

\subsection{CNN Training and Evaluation}

The network was trained to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) using the adaptive moment estimation (Adam) optimizer. Five-fold cross-validation was used: in each fold, four folds were used for training and the remaining fold for testing, corresponding to an 80:20 train-test split. The model was trained for 40 epochs in each fold, with 350 batches of 32 samples per epoch for a total time of training per fold near to 1.2 hours.

Before training, the target values for X, Y, and Z were normalized. Min-max normalization scaled each value to the range $[0,1]$ to match the network's output range. An additional normalization step was applied to X and Y because the points lie along a circular trajectory. The robotic arm's rotational movement in constant 10-degree increments means that the projections of these coordinates are expressed as multiples of sine and cosine functions. Consequently, the coordinate values are not uniformly distributed. The arcsine transformation shown in Equation \ref{eq:normalization} was therefore applied to obtain an equidistant mapping.


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:normalization}
\text{label\_arcnorm} =  \frac{\arcsin(2\text{label\_norm} - 1) + \frac{\pi}{2}}{\pi}
\end{equation}

After training, the model was switched to evaluation mode, and the test set was processed to obtain the estimator's predictions. The inverse of the transformation in Equation \ref{eq:normalization} was applied to denormalize the predictions. Estimation errors were then calculated as the differences between the predicted and ground-truth coordinates. The mean absolute error (MAE), error distribution, and percentage of predictions within each error range were calculated from the test results.


\section{ESN Estimator}

Echo State Networks (ESNs) are well suited to sequential data because their rich temporal memory and nonlinear dynamics enable them to learn complex patterns, as in recurrent neural networks (RNNs). Unlike conventional RNNs, however, ESNs do not require training the recurrent part of the network. They also tend to be compact, making them a memory-efficient choice for the estimator proposed in this thesis.

\subsection{Mel spectrogram as sequence}

For the ESN, each audio channel is cropped to 20,000 samples to remove the low-energy tail of the signal. The cropped signals are then transformed into sequences of Mel-spectrum values using a process similar to that described in the CNN subsection on Log-Mel spectrograms. To reduce computation time, the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) uses a window length of 1,024 samples and a hop size of 512 samples, yielding 40 frames. A Mel filter bank compresses the data into 128 bands, a resolution adequate for acoustic scene and direction-of-arrival identification \cite{numelbands}. The resulting bands are converted to decibels, producing a sequence of 40 time steps with 128 features per channel. Finally, min-max normalization is applied to the input features to mitigate issues caused by large differences in magnitude.

\subsection{ESN Architecture}

The development of the ESN architecture begins with the vanilla flavor of the ESN all set to work as an clasifier. for exploration and evaluation the network is first configure as an clasifier due that this task is easier than regression \cite{vapnikstatistical}, so the earlier evaluations and changes on the ESN are conducted in it clasification mode. 

The base ESN had as input four mel spectograms sequences of 128 bands each, for a total of 512 inputs, the neurons use tanh activation function for reservoir and the feedforward network. The output softmax layer clasifiy into 26 classes based on the session in which the audio is recorded.

The spectral radius that control how memory decay in the reservoir is desire to have it as hier as posible in order that the network present a long term memory behaviour, the maximun value recommended had a boundary of 1 to ensure the ESP to warranty that the internal states of the reservoir will converge over time avoiding instability. For the ESN presented in this work a value of 0.99 was choose as spectral radius for the reservoir matrix. The leaky rate also controlls how fast the reservoir forget and it set to 0.1 to avoid memory loss and keep as much as posible of the history of the input sequence in the reservoir.

Sparsity was set to 0.1 to maintain sufficient connectivity among the reservoir neurons. Because reservoir size affects both memory use during training and the number of trainable weights in the feedforward network, a search was conducted to identify a favorable balance between accuracy and model size. After 1,200 training epochs, the ESN estimator achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of approximately 2 cm. The error-range percentages are reported in Table \ref{tab:esn_error_comparison}. To further reduce the MAE, the architecture was extended with a multihead feedforward network. This change was motivated by the ESN's performance as a classifier: it could distinguish recording sessions, including sessions with nearby positions. However, in the regression task, the estimation errors were sometimes larger than the distance between adjacent sessions, suggesting that the network had difficulty learning the mapping from reservoir features to output coordinates.

The classifier used a separate output for each class, allowing it to learn a distinct mapping for each class. Inspired by this approach, the multihead network divides coordinate prediction into separate tasks: one head predicts positive coordinate values, another predicts negative values, and a third predicts the coordinate sign. The sign-prediction head selects which of the positive or negative value heads provides the coordinate estimate. This approach substantially improved the ESN estimator's performance, but occasional sign-prediction errors still caused large estimation errors. To address this issue, a fourth head was added to estimate the coordinate across its full range. Although this head is less accurate on its own, its prediction serves as a reference for the other heads. If its prediction differs from the selected positive or negative value prediction by more than a threshold (a difference exceeds 100 cm, indicating a sign-classification outlier error), the model treats this as a possible sign-prediction error and selects the estimate from the opposite-sign head instead.The hybrid decision logic is shown in Algorithm \ref{alg:hybrid_decision_logic}.

For the sign and full range heads, the feedforward networks contains 60,000 trainable parameters each. The positive and negative coordinate heads that requiere to be more acurate have 120,000 trainable parameters each. This results in a total of 360,000 trainable parameters for the multihead feedforward network what is only near to 3\% of the ResNet-18 CNN parameters. The multihead feedforward network architecture is shown in Figure \ref{fig:multihead}. Its results, including an MAE of approximately 0.7 cm and improved error-range percentages, are reported in Table \ref{tab:esn_error_comparison}.

\subsection{Multi-Head ESN Training and Evaluation}

Each feedforward head was trained independently using a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$. The positive and negative coordinate heads were trained only on samples with the corresponding sign, whereas the overall-coordinate and sign-prediction heads were trained on all samples. The positive, negative, and overall-coordinate heads were trained with the Adam optimizer for 400 epochs. The sign-prediction head was trained for 800 epochs using binary cross-entropy with logits loss. The total training time for each fold was approximately 1.5 hours.

The models were trained and evaluated using five-fold cross-validation: four folds were used for training and one for testing in each iteration, corresponding to an 80:20 split. The target values for X, Y, and Z were normalized as described in Section 5.1.3. The network outputs were then denormalized to obtain the final coordinate estimates.
 


\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Hybrid Decision Logic for Coordinate Estimation with Outlier Mitigation}
\label{alg:hybrid_decision_logic}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Feature vector $\mathbf{x}$, Sign Head $\hat{s}(\cdot)$, Positive Head $\hat{y}_{+}(\cdot)$, Negative Head $\hat{y}_{-}(\cdot)$, Overall Estimator $\hat{y}_{\text{overall}}(\cdot)$, Outlier Threshold $\tau = 100\text{ cm}$
\Ensure Final Coordinate Estimation $\hat{y}$

\Statex \textbf{// Phase 1: Sign Classification}
\State $\text{pred\_sign} \gets \hat{s}(\mathbf{x})$ \Comment{Predicts whether target is positive or negative}

\Statex \textbf{// Phase 2: Conditional Path Selection}
\If{$\text{pred\_sign} \ge 0$} \Comment{Positive condition branch}
    \State $\hat{y}_{\text{cand}} \gets \hat{y}_{+}(\mathbf{x})$ \Comment{Candidate conditional path prediction}
    \State $\hat{y}_{\text{alt}} \gets \hat{y}_{-}(\mathbf{x})$  \Comment{Alternate path for fallback}
\Else \Comment{Negative condition branch}
    \State $\hat{y}_{\text{cand}} \gets \hat{y}_{-}(\mathbf{x})$
    \State $\hat{y}_{\text{alt}} \gets \hat{y}_{+}(\mathbf{x})$
\EndIf

\Statex \textbf{// Phase 3: Outlier Mitigation and Fallback Logic}
\State $\hat{y}_{\text{ref}} \gets \hat{y}_{\text{overall}}(\mathbf{x})$ \Comment{Evaluate baseline overall estimator}
\State $\Delta \gets |\hat{y}_{\text{ref}} - \hat{y}_{\text{cand}}|$ \Comment{Compute absolute discrepancy}

\If{$\Delta > \tau$} \Comment{Potential critical sign classification failure detected}
    \State $\hat{y} \gets \hat{y}_{\text{alt}}$ \Comment{Fallback to alternate estimator output}
\Else
    \State $\hat{y} \gets \hat{y}_{\text{cand}}$ \Comment{Retain candidate conditional path prediction}
\EndIf

\State \Return $\hat{y}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The microphones are arranged in pairs in a plane parallel to the X-Z plane, enabling binaural recording. Measurements along the X and Z axes can therefore be used to evaluate the effect of beamforming on position estimation. Figure \ref{fig:geometrybbox} shows the midpoints of the sides perpendicular to the X-axis, which are treated as potential source locations for the beamforming procedure. To model sound arriving at both microphones from these locations or nearby regions, the difference between the two acoustic path lengths must be calculated. Using trigonometry, the distance from point E to point G is 0.522\,m, while the distance from E to point H is 0.9862\,m. The difference, 0.4642\,m, is the additional distance the sound wave travels to reach point H. Assuming a speed of sound of 346\,m/s—the approximate value at 26\,$^{\circ}$C \cite{audiospeed}, which is close to the average temperature at the testing site—the time difference of arrival (TDOA) between the microphones is 1.34\,ms. As shown in Equation \ref{eq:Fa0}, this value serves as the reference delay for beamforming. Applying this delay to one microphone signal aligns the signals for constructive interference and maximizes the coherent summation of the desired sound wave. According to Equation \ref{eqn:ddipole}, given a microphone separation of 0.7\,m, the minimum frequency for beamforming is 123.57\,Hz. Figure \ref{fig:resfreqaudio} and Table \ref{tab:mainfreq} show that the stimulus contains its main frequency components above this threshold, making beamforming feasible in this setup.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:Fa0}
 \Delta t_{\text{arrival}} = \frac{0.4642\,\text{m}}{346\,\text{m/s}} = 1.34\,\text{ms}
\end{equation}

As an example, selecting the highest-magnitude components around 400\,Hz for beamforming, combined with the computed time difference of arrival, enables an analysis of how the signal's main spectral components behave over the travel time $\Delta t$. At 400\,Hz, the signal period is 2.5\,ms. For a propagation delay $\Delta t = 1.34\,\text{ms}$ (as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:ondas400}), the acoustic wave arrives at point G in an inverted-phase condition relative to point H, causing destructive cancellation when the two signals are summed. Consequently, introducing a delay equal 1.34\,\text{ms} to the signal at point G produces an alignemet of the signals resulting in constructive interference for signal when added to the signal from point H at 400\,Hz. Furthermore, as the reverberant source shifts away from this target location, destructive attenuation penalizes the gain at these frequencies, fulfilling the objective of the beamforming operation.
