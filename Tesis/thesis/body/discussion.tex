\section{CNN model and the regression problem}

\subsection{CNN model to identify cube regions and orientations}

Following the problem-complexity tiers described by \citeauthor{vapnikstatistical} in \cite{vapnikstatistical}, the model was first evaluated as a classifier with the task of determine the cube's location within some regions and the cube orientation. For the location-classification task across the acoustic sessions, the CNN achieved 98.59\% accuracy on the test set. This result indicates that the audio signals, once transformed into spectrograms, contain sufficient information for spatial localization and that the network can extract the features needed to identify the cube's region within the measurement box. The confusion matrix in Figure \ref{fig:cnn_confusion_matrix} shows a clear diagonal pattern, indicating strong classification performance. Twenty of the 26 classes achieved 100\% recall. Most errors occurred between neighboring classes; class 1 had the highest number of misclassifications, with 13 samples classified as class 0, resulting in 75\% accuracy for that particular class.

In the orientation-classification task, the network was required to assign the cube's orientation to one of nine classes and achieved 29.06\% accuracy on the test set. Unlike the location-classification results, the confusion matrix in Figure \ref{fig:cnn_confusion_matrix_orientation} does not show a clear diagonal pattern. Instead, it reveals three clusters of misclassifications near the diagonal. In the first cluster, samples from classes 0, 1, and 2 are often classified as class 1. In the middle cluster, samples from classes 2, 3, 4, and 5 are often classified as class 4. In the rightmost cluster, samples from classes 5, 6, 7, and 8 are often classified as class 6. Overall, predictions are concentrated in classes 1 (10 degrees), 4 (40 degrees), and 6 (60 degrees). This pattern suggests that the network tends to favor a few broad orientation categories spaced by arround 20 to 30-degree rather than reliably distinguishing all nine classes, which represent 10-degree increments.

The further exploration to analize the efect of the proximity of the cube to the microphones and the relative location of its front face  with respect to the sides of the measure box, the acuracy by regions shows in figure \ref{tab:regional_accuracy} tells that the performance is not improved by the proximity or the reducton of variability with respect of the cube location with respect to it location inside the measurement box. The fact that the accuracy yield even lower can be explain by the reduction of samples avaliable for training in those reduced regions. One aspect to highlight for the experiment of clasification into the subregios is that even though the acurracy were low, the confusion matrix of figure \ref{fig:conf_matrix_3regions} shows better distribution of samples along the matrix diagonal. 

Comparing the two tasks, the results show a marked difference in performance: spatial-location classification achieved over 98\% accuracy, whereas orientation classification achieved only 29.06\%. This difference may arise because rotating the cube changes the orientation of its surfaces without substantially changing their positions relative to the microphones and the wall in the box. Consequently, orientation may be encoded in subtle inter-channel phase shifts or in the attenuation of certain echoes, rather than in pronounced Time-Difference-Of-Arrival (TDOA) cues. By contrast, translating the cube changes the source–microphone geometry and may produce more distinct TDOA cues and changes in the reverberation pattern. The CNN may therefore have difficulty isolating orientation-dependent features in the spectrograms, which could explain why its predictions are concentrated in a few orientation classes.

\subsection{CNN model to estimate the cube location}

In the case of estimating the cube's location for the individual coordinates X, Y and Z using the CNN model, the figures \ref{fig:five_fold_X_histo}, \ref{fig:five_fold_Y_histo}, and \ref{fig:five_fold_Z_histo} present the histograms of the prediction errors for the X, Y, and Z coordinates obtained from the five folds. The histograms indicate that the model is generally able to localize the cube with reasonable accuracy, with most of the errors falling within a narrow range of values arround $\pm 2$ cm, narrow relative to the dimension of the measurement box and the cube size. Most errors are concentrated around zero, suggesting that the network's predictions are close to the true positions. However, there are occasional larger errors, which may be attributed to challenging acoustic conditions like noise during recording. The X and Y histograms show comparable results, for Z coordinate estimation the fold 1 and fold 4 present wider distributions with more errors above the range of $\pm 2$ cm that indicated the Z estimation is more challenging for the network to estimate the coordinates. Overall, the CNN demonstrates a strong capability for spatial localization, consistent with the high classification accuracy observed in the previous section, indicating that the CNN is able to accurately estimate the cube's position in all three dimensions.

The MAE is the main figure of merit to evaluate the estimation capability of the models \cite{friedmanrecomendation}. The table \ref{tab:mae_xyz_folds} summarizes the mean and standard deviation of the prediction errors for the X, Y, and Z coordinates across the five folds. The results indicate that the CNN achieves the lowest errors for Y coordinates, followed by X. For these two coordinates the network performs similarly well, with MAEs arround 0.5 cm. In the case of Z the mean MAE almost double the values for X and Y with a MAE of 0.9 cm and bigger standard deviation. The results in the table are consistent with the observations from the histograms. This further supports the conclusion that the network is more accurate in estimating the horizontal positions of the cube than its vertical position.

To understand the distribution of errors by tiers, the table \ref{tab:error_distribution_xyz} present the percentage of samples in the dataset with estimation error within a especific error range. The results are congruent with the previous paragraph: Y is the most accurately estimated coordinate: $92.90\%$ of samples fall within $\pm 1\text{ cm}$ of error, reaching $98.32\%$ within $\pm 2\text{ cm}$ on average across the 5 folds. X exhibits high precision with $87.69\%$ of samples fall within $\pm 1\text{ cm}$, rising rapidly to value comparable to Y of $97.83\%$ within $\pm 2\text{ cm}$.   $Z$ presents the highest fine-scale error and inter-fold variance: Only $64.44\%$ of samples achieve estimation errors within $\pm 1\text{ cm}$, and that the range of $\pm 3\text{ cm}$. is the one to have a comparable value of $98.32\%$, that presenting this network 8.92\% of errors values above $\pm 2\text{ cm}$. For the three CNN estimators, errors exceeding $\pm 4\text{ cm}$ represent less than $0.6\%$ of all samples ($X$: $99.42\%$, $Y$: $99.69\%$, $Z$: $99.45\%$). 

Other figure of merit to evaluate regresion is the Pearson Correlation Coefficients \cite{friedmanrecomendation} shown in table \ref{tab:pearson_corr_xyz} for all the folds in the three coordinates with the exeption of fold 3 on Z coordinate (a value of 0.9898), the coeficient is greater than 0.99 for the rest of the cases, indicating a strong linear relationship between the predicted and true coordinates. This further confirms the network's capability to accurately estimate the cube's position in the measurement box.

The error distributions across the three dimensions are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:plot3d_errors}, which shows the errors in the X, Y, and Z coordinates throughout the measurement box. The visualization indicates that errors are distributed relatively uniformly across the measurement space. Most errors are close to zero, while larger errors occur in a few regions; however, no clear clustering pattern is apparent. The figure \ref{fig:plot2d_errors} present the error for each coordinate only as function of it own cordinate value. In the case of X absolute estimate errors as a function of the X coordinate can be observe that the error remains low (near 2cm) in the central area and increase near the edges, with the boundary concentrating the estimation outliers. For Y a similar behaviour is observed, the center of the axis present lower values than the edge, but the error values in the center are even lower than the obtained in the estimation of X, this suggest that the task of estimating the coordinate on the Y axis is easier for the network compared to estimating magnitudes in the X domain. This aspect is particularly interesting due the fact that for the X estimation, the network is trained with a binaural input channels what allow the network to exploit the physical separation between two sound receivers, capable to learn interaural time difference from the channels, but the Y estimation perform bettern than the X. The fact that Y estimation have a strong relationship to the distance the cube is located in the path from the acoustic signal from the speaker source to the receiver microphones and this add a distintive interference effect when the cube is near the speakers or near the microphones that could explaing why the estimation along this axis present lower errors than X. For the estimation of Z, the errors are higher than X and Y. One possible explanation is that the Z-coordinate values are not uniformly distributed around zero: the dataset contains more positive values than negative ones. Consequently, the network is trained on more samples in the range from zero to 40 than in the negative range. This imbalance may contribute to the larger estimation errors observed for negative Z values.
