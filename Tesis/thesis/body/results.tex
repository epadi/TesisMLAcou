This chapter presents the results obtained from the evaluation of the proposed models for acoustic inferencing tasks. First, the models are evaluated on their ability to classify the acoustic location of the cube inside the measurement enviroment. Then, a more broad evaluation is conducted where the models are assessed on their ability to do regression and properly estimate the location of the object inside the measurement box. For the regresion results, the figures of merit are presented for each model on each spatial dimension they are traing to predict. Finally a probabilistic evaluation is conducted to compare the performance of the models against each other.


\section{CNN for Acoustic Classification}

The first model trained and evaluated is the Resnet18 CNN, this network is powerfull for image classification tasks \cite{Resnetwhy} and it used as the reference model for the acoustic inferencing tasks presented in this work.

\subsection{CNN Model for Acoustic to Spatial Location Classification}

The CNN was trained on a classification task where it had to classify the acoustic location of the sound source from the 26 possible locations, each one corresponding to a specific recording session characterized by the same X, and Y location and only changing the Z location. The network was trained on 20 epochs. After the training the network shows a accuracy of 99.4\% on training and 98.59\% on the test set. The confusion matrix is shown in Figure \ref{fig:cnn_confusion_matrix}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[height=15cm, width=15cm]{../img/clasification_location_cnn.png}
        \caption{Confusion matrix for the evaluation of the CNN for the acoustic location classification task.}
        \label{fig:cnn_confusion_matrix}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

\subsection{CNN Model for Acoustic to Orientation Classification}

The second evaluation tested the CNN's ability to classify the cube's orientation among nine possible orientations. For each orientation, the cube was rotated by 10 degrees about one of its centroidal axes. The network was trained for 20 epochs and achieved an accuracy of 99.0\% on the training set and 29.06\% on the test set. The confusion matrix is shown in Figure \ref{fig:cnn_confusion_matrix_orientation}; each class represents a cube orientation that differs from the others by 10 degrees.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[height=10cm, width=10cm]{../img/clasification_orientation_cnn.png}
        \caption{Confusion matrix for CNN acoustic orientation classification on test set.}
        \label{fig:cnn_confusion_matrix_orientation}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

For both models using beamforming, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), the Pearson correlation coefficient, and absolute error distribution grahics are reported in this section. The detailed fold-wise histograms and the percentages of samples within the estimation error ranges are provided in the appendix.

\section{Model Comparison}

To compare the models' performance in estimating the spatial coordinates, the MAE values for each fold, along with their means and standard deviations, are presented for the $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ coordinates in Tables \ref{tab:mae_x_models_comparison}, \ref{tab:mae_y_models_comparison}, and \ref{tab:mae_z_models_comparison}, respectively. The fold-wise MAE values are used to rank the models for each coordinate. These rankings are then used as input to the Bayesian Plackett Luce model, whose posterior distribution is sampled using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).

The Plackett--Luce likelihood was discounted by a factor of $(1 + K\rho)^{-1}$, where $\rho = 0.25$ represents the ratio of the test-set size to the training-set size in our five-fold setup. This correlation penalty effectively scales the posterior probability distribution, providing a more conservative estimate of the probability that a given architecture is the best model for estimating each coordinate ($X$, $Y$, and $Z$).
